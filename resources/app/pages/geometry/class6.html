2. Chapter 2 — Lines and Angles

Point CBSE-C6-GE-010

A point marks a location (no length or width)

A point is an exact position. It has no size. We name points with capital letters, such as A, B, or P. Think of the tip of a sharp pencil on paper.

Line CBSE-C6-GE-011

A line extends endlessly in both directions

A straight line has no endpoints and infinite length. A line through A and B is written as line AB.

Ray CBSE-C6-GE-012

A ray has one endpoint and extends in one direction

Ray AB starts at A and passes through B without end. A sunbeam is a useful picture of a ray.

Line segment CBSE-C6-GE-013

A line segment joins two endpoints and has fixed length

Segment AB is the part of a line between A and B. The edge of a ruler between two marks is a line segment.

Parallel lines CBSE-C6-GE-014

Parallel lines never meet and stay the same distance apart

Two lines in a plane that do not meet, however far you extend them, are parallel. Opposite edges of a ruler are parallel.

Intersecting lines CBSE-C6-GE-015

Intersecting lines meet at exactly one point

Two lines that cross form angles at their intersection point. The letter X is formed by intersecting line segments.

Example A — Point, line, ray, segment

Name the object: an exact location with no size; a path that goes on forever both ways; a path with one start and no end the other way; a path with two ends and a length.

Location with no size is a point. Endless both ways is a line. One endpoint, one endless direction is a ray. Two endpoints is a line segment.

Point, line, ray, line segment.

Example B — Parallel and intersecting

Two rails of a straight railway track never meet. Two roads cross at a junction.

Rails that never meet are parallel. Roads that cross are intersecting.

Parallel lines; intersecting lines.

Right angle CBSE-C6-GE-001

Right angle = 90°

An angle that measures exactly 90° is a right angle. The corner of a square page is a right angle.

Acute angle CBSE-C6-GE-016

0° < acute angle < 90°

An acute angle is smaller than a right angle. 45° is an acute angle.

Obtuse angle CBSE-C6-GE-017

90° < obtuse angle < 180°

An obtuse angle is larger than a right angle but smaller than a straight angle. 120° is an obtuse angle.

Straight angle CBSE-C6-GE-002

Straight angle = 180°

An angle that forms a straight line measures 180°.

Reflex angle CBSE-C6-GE-018

180° < reflex angle < 360°

A reflex angle is greater than a straight angle and less than a complete angle. 270° is a reflex angle.

Complete angle CBSE-C6-GE-003

Complete angle = 360°

A complete angle is one full turn about a point. Four right angles make one complete angle.

Complementary angles CBSE-C6-GE-004

∠A + ∠B = 90°

Two angles are complementary if their measures add to 90°. They need not sit next to each other. If ∠A = 35°, the complement is 90° − 35° = 55°. The Geometry engine does not compute complements; learn this from the formula and the example below.

Supplementary angles CBSE-C6-GE-005

∠A + ∠B = 180°

Two angles are supplementary if their measures add to 180°. Angles on a straight line are supplementary. If ∠A = 110°, the supplement is 180° − 110° = 70°. The Geometry engine does not compute supplements; learn this from the formula and the example below.

Example C — Types of angles

Classify 45°, 90°, 120°, 180°, 270°, and 360°.

Compare each measure with 90°, 180°, and 360°.

Acute, right, obtuse, straight, reflex, complete.

Example D — Complementary angles

Find the complement of 48°.

Complement = 90° − 48° = 42°. Check: 48° + 42° = 90°.

42°

Example E — Supplementary angles

Find the supplement of 125°.

Supplement = 180° − 125° = 55°. Check: 125° + 55° = 180°.

55°

Triangle basics CBSE-C6-GE-019

Triangle: 3 sides, 3 vertices, 3 angles; angle sum = 180°

A triangle is a closed figure made of three line segments. Classify by sides: equilateral (all equal), isosceles (two equal), scalene (all different). Classify by angles: acute (all angles < 90°), right (one angle = 90°), obtuse (one angle > 90°). Perimeter and area of a triangle are Mensuration, not Geometry on this page.

Example F — Classify by sides

Classify the triangle with sides 5, 5, 5; then 5, 5, 8; then 3, 4, 5.

All three equal → equilateral. Exactly two equal → isosceles. All different → scalene.

Equilateral; isosceles; scalene.

Example G — Classify by angles

Classify the triangle with angles 90°, 45°, 45°; then 40°, 60°, 80°; then 20°, 30°, 130°.

First check the sum is 180°. One 90° → right. All below 90° → acute. One above 90° → obtuse.

Right triangle; acute triangle; obtuse triangle.

3. Chapter 8 — Playing with Constructions

Circle defined by radius CBSE-C6-GE-006

A circle is the set of all points at fixed distance r from centre O

With compass and ruler, keep a constant opening (the radius) from the centre. If the radius is 4 cm, every point on the circle is 4 cm from the centre. Class 6 focuses on this construction idea, not on circumference or area formulas.

Perpendicular bisector property CBSE-C6-GE-007

Any point on the perpendicular bisector of AB is equidistant from A and B

The perpendicular bisector meets a segment at its midpoint at 90°. If M is the midpoint of AB and line ℓ is perpendicular to AB at M, then for any point P on ℓ, PA = PB.

These two ideas are taught here as Study Material only. The Geometry engine does not solve construction questions, and Try It does not include them.

Example H — Radius of a circle

If OP = 5 cm for every point P on a circle with centre O, find the radius.

Every point on the circle is at the same distance from the centre. That distance is the radius.

5 cm

Example I — Perpendicular bisector

Where does the perpendicular bisector meet AB?

It meets the segment at the midpoint, at 90°.

At the midpoint, at 90°.

4. Chapter 9 — Symmetry

Line of symmetry CBSE-C6-GE-008

A line of symmetry divides a figure into two mirror-image halves

If one half of a figure matches the other half across a line, that line is a line of symmetry. A square has 4 lines of symmetry. A non-equilateral isosceles triangle has 1. Some figures have none.

Order of rotational symmetry CBSE-C6-GE-009

Order = 360° ÷ (smallest angle of turn that maps the figure onto itself)

Rotational symmetry exists when a figure looks the same after a turn less than 360° about its centre. A square matches every 90°, so order = 360° ÷ 90° = 4. Every figure has order at least 1.

Symmetry is taught here as Study Material only. The Geometry engine does not solve symmetry questions. Do not type words such as “rotation” or “reflection” into Try It — those are treated as unsupported advanced topics.

Example J — Lines of symmetry

How many lines of symmetry does an equilateral triangle have?

Each altitude through a vertex to the opposite side is a line of symmetry. There are three vertices.

3

Example K — Order of rotational symmetry

A regular hexagon matches itself every 60°. Find its order.

Order = 360° ÷ 60° = 6.

6. Common mistakes

A line is not a line segment. A line has no ends; a segment has two ends and a length.

A ray has one endpoint, not two. Naming order matters: ray AB starts at A.

Parallel lines never meet. Intersecting lines meet at exactly one point.

Acute is less than 90°. Obtuse is between 90° and 180°. Do not call 90° acute or obtuse.

Complementary sums to 90°. Supplementary sums to 180°. Do not mix the two.

Triangle interior angles must add to 180° before you classify by angles.

Do not use this page for perimeter or area. Those are Mensuration.
